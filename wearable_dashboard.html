01 · The data

A large synthetic wearable dataset

Every record below was generated by code, not collected from real people. Names are already removed, the way most "anonymized" datasets are released. What remains: age, gender, pincode, heart rate, steps, sleep and a health condition.

02 · The attack

No names, but still traceable

Suppose an attacker already knows three ordinary facts about someone (from social media, a gym profile, a delivery address): their age, gender and pincode. They look those up in the dataset.

03 · The defence

Generalize, then suppress

k-anonymity means every person sits in a group of at least 5 people who look identical in the data (themselves included). We blur age and pincode step by step. The more we blur, the safer people are, and the less detail researchers keep. Pick a level to see it.

04 · Differential privacy

Noise instead of blurring

Differential privacy adds calibrated random noise, controlled by a number called epsilon. Smaller epsilon means more noise and stronger privacy. There are two ways to use it on heart-rate data, and the accuracy cost differs a lot.

05 · Scorecard

Device privacy scorecard

Each device is scored on eight criteria from the literature review: encryption at rest and in transit, two-factor authentication, consent, retention disclosure, data deletion, BLE transparency, and third-party sharing.

These five device profiles are illustrative inputs I chose to demonstrate the scoring model. They are not audited against each company's current privacy policy. The point values are my own weighting, not taken from a published standard.

06 · Verification

Automated tests

The Python code is checked by automated unit tests (Python's unittest). Each one verifies a single behaviour, so a mistake in the scoring, k-anonymity or noise code would show up as a failure.

These results were recorded by Python when it ran the analysis; the tests run in Python, not in the browser.
