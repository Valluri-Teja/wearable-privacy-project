A large real dataset: thousands of people

This page repeats the privacy tests on public data from the US CDC's NHANES survey: thousands of real people who wore a wrist accelerometer, with their real age, gender, race, education and income.

01 — The source

Public wearable data from a national health survey

NHANES is run by the US CDC. In 2013–2014, participants wore a research accelerometer on the wrist for up to seven days. The activity summaries and the demographic details of the same people are published openly.

02 — The data

What we are working with

Activity is measured in MIMS units, a standard measure of wrist movement. A day counts only if the device was worn at least hours, and a person is used only if they have enough valid days.

03 — k-anonymity

How identifiable are real people from their demographics?

An attacker who knows someone's age, gender, race, education and income can look for the one matching row. We make the data less detailed in five steps and count how many people end up alone or in a group smaller than .

04 — The attack

Can an activity pattern find the same person again?

Each person's valid days are split into two halves. The attacker holds the first-half summaries of everyone, with no names. For a person's second-half summary, they look for the most similar first-half summary. Then we check whether they found the right person. We test two attackers: one who uses activity only, and one who also knows the person's age band, gender and race.

05 — Differential privacy

What noise costs with a big crowd

We release the average daily activity with random noise (Laplace noise, controlled by epsilon), protecting one whole person. The same promise costs far less accuracy when many people are in the data.
