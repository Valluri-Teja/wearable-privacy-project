How private is the thing on your wrist, really?

A working demo built for this project — it scores wearable devices against the risk criteria from the literature review, then shows exactly why anonymization on its own isn't the safeguard it looks like. Backed by 13 automated tests.

01 — Scoring

Privacy risk scorecard

Five devices are scored against eight criteria pulled from the reviewed literature — encryption at rest and in transit, two-factor authentication, consent flow, third-party sharing, and more. The score decides whether a device lands as low, medium, or high risk.

03 — Differential privacy

The Laplace mechanism

Generalization hides someone inside a group, but the numeric readings themselves (like heart rate) can still be revealing. Differential privacy adds carefully calibrated random noise instead — controlled by a parameter called epsilon. Smaller epsilon means stronger privacy and more noise; larger epsilon means weaker privacy but data closer to the truth.

Illustrative values shown for the first five users. The Python script generates fresh noise on every run, so its numbers will differ slightly each time.

This is the privacy–utility trade-off discussed in Saifuzzaman et al. (2021) and demonstrated practically in Benouis et al. (2024): protecting individuals more heavily makes the released data less accurate for analysis.

04 — Verification

Automated test suite

The Python implementation ships with 13 automated unit tests (built with Python's unittest module). Each test checks one specific behaviour, so any mistake in the scoring, k-anonymity or noise logic would show up as a failure. All 13 pass.

These results come from running python -m unittest test_wearable_privacy_demo.py -v on the accompanying Python file. They are shown here as a summary of that run; the tests themselves execute in Python, not in the browser.
