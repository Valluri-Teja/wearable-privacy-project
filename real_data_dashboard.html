The main dashboard used synthetic users. This page repeats the privacy tests on a public dataset of real Fitbit heart-rate readings: how much accuracy differential privacy costs, and whether "anonymous" people can still be found again.

01 · The source

A public dataset of real Fitbit users

The data comes from volunteers who agreed to share their Fitbit tracker data for research. It is published openly, so it can be used here as long as the authors are credited.

02 · The data

Millions of real heart-rate readings

Each reading is a person's heart rate, recorded every few seconds. Only an anonymous number identifies the person: no name, age or location.

Average heart rate per person (number of people in each range, beats per minute)

03 · The attack

Can a heart-rate pattern identify someone?

An attacker holds records from the first period and from the second period. For each person in the first, they look for the second-period record with the most similar heart-rate pattern across the 24 hours of a day, without using the anonymous number. Then we check whether they found the right person.

04 · Differential privacy

What noise costs on real data

We release the average heart rate with random noise added (Laplace noise, controlled by epsilon). There are two levels of promise: hiding one single reading, or hiding one whole person. They give very different accuracy.
